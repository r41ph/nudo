Step 02 — Redesign

Three directions

All three fix the contrast failures, the undersized targets and the hover-only destructive actions — those aren't strategy, they're defects. Where they differ is what they believe the outline is for. Each shows the same three screens: a document open, the outline's row menu open, and focus mode. Reference them in chat as 1a, 1b, 1c.

1a
Conservative

Same bones, repaired

Identical layout DNA — rail, top bar, page. Every change is a token value, a target size, or a component swap. The one structural move: the page loses its card and gains its own title, so the document stops being anonymous.

Key decisions
1

Rows go to 34px and hover becomes a 3.5% ink wash, not a terracotta tint — the accent is now reserved for the one active row (fill plus a 2.5px left bar). Hovering four rows no longer looks like four selections.

2

“New” states its scope in the label. The footer button reads New in Chapter One and follows selection, so the root-versus-child guess disappears.

3

One save state at minute precision in a token colour, and the day's words shown against a target you can set. The cluster drops from three colours to one.

4

The toolbar becomes a fixed row — FixedToolbar is already vendored and commented out. Headings work on an empty line, and “Customise” is finally visible.

5

No card, no shadow, no fixed bottom edge. The document's title is a real H1 in the page, so the breadcrumb only has to carry ancestors.

6

“Story” drops to 10.5px and gains a 26px add button beside it. The group label stops outranking the chapters underneath it.

Trade-off

Cheapest to ship and nothing to relearn — most of it is token values and two component swaps. But it does nothing for planning: this is still a file tree with a word count, and the questions “where am I in the book” and “what's unwritten” stay unanswered. 1b answers them.

Key decisions
1

A 56px icon rail takes the app-level controls — project, search, settings, theme, account — off the outline panel and out of the top bar. Both were carrying jobs that weren't theirs.

2

“+0 today” becomes a real instrument: a daily target with a bar. The number the current top bar shows exists precisely so it can be measured against something.

3

Chapters are bands, not rows: a raised block with title, status word and a progress bar against a chapter target. The two-pixel type difference stops carrying the hierarchy — the surface does.

4

Status becomes a word, not a 9px moon dot. The dot was elegant and unreadable; “Drafting” costs 60px and needs no legend.

5

Search moves into the panel with ⌘K. There is no search in the product today, and it's the first thing a writer with 60,000 words wants.

6

A 52px margin strip surfaces comments and suggestions. The whole discussion system is implemented and has no visible entry point — this gives it one without a full sidebar.

Trade-off

Answers questions 1a can't — where am I, what's unwritten, where is that scene — and surfaces three features already built. The cost is real: 368px of chrome instead of 244px, a rewritten rail, and targets set by a goal the writer now has to choose. If they don't want goals, the spine is decoration.

Key decisions
1

Board / Write is the only global mode switch, and it replaces focus mode, the rail toggle and the theme switch as separate concerns. Two states, one control.

2

A chapter is a column heading, not a row you expand. Nothing collapses, nothing indents, nothing truncates at fourteen characters — the structure is the layout.

3

Cards carry a synopsis line. It's the one piece of planning data the current model has no room for, and it's what makes a board worth looking at instead of a list.

4

The editor is the same card, enlarged — the terracotta ring travels with it. There is no separate editor surface to design, and no navigation to lose your place in.

5

Paper cards on a dark board give dark mode a reason to exist: the prose is always ink on paper, so contrast never inverts. The board itself is the only thing that changes theme.

Trade-off

This is the direction that actually solves what you don't like, by removing it. It's also the most expensive: a card model, a synopsis field, drag-between-columns, and a zoom transition — none of which exist. And a board beats a list at 40 scenes and loses badly at 300, where scrolling sideways through nine chapters is worse than a tree ever was. 1b is the safer version of the same ambition.
